Pi Fought the Law and Pi Won

Overweening governments sometimes attempt to dictate scientific and mathematical truth by fiat. How might that work out for them? Let’s use a case from our very own United States as an example.

In 1897, the Indiana House of Representatives voted 67-0 in favor of a bill that implied pi equals 3.2. How far off is that value?

  1. The bill states that “the ratio of the diameter and circumference is as five-fourths to four.” What value of pi does that give?
  2. A circular path has a diameter of 100 feet. How long is the path using 3.2? Using π? How far apart are the answers?
  3. Find the percent error of 3.2 as a value of π.
  4. The same bill says the ratio of a square’s diagonal to its side is “as ten to seven.” Use the Pythagorean theorem to find the true ratio. How close did the bill come?

Answers

  1. 4 ÷ 5/4 = 3.2.
  2. 320 feet using 3.2; about 314.2 feet using π. Difference is about 5.8 feet.
  3. ~1.9% too high.
  4. √2, about 1.414. Ten to seven is about 1.429, ~1% too high.

Have you ever heard of squaring the circle? This mathematical puzzle challenges you to construct a square with the same area as a given circle by using only a finite number of steps with a compass and straightedge. Spoiler: this is impossible, as pi is a transcendental number. You cannot square a circle, but that doesn’t stop cranks from trying.

The bill came from Edward (some sources call him Edwin) J. Goodwin, a physician from Solitude, Indiana, who believed he had squared the circle (he hadn’t). In his infinite generosity, he wanted to present his “new mathematical truth” to Indiana for free, while everyone else would have to pay him royalties.

C.A. Waldo, a Purdue mathematics professor, happened to be at the statehouse that week. By his own account he declined to meet Goodwin, and by that evening “the senators were properly coached.” On February 12, 1897, the Senate postponed the bill indefinitely. It never became law.

Years prior, in 1882, a mathematician proved that no one can square the circle. How do you prove an impossibility?

Sources

Edington, Will E. “House Bill No. 246, Indiana State Legislature, 1897.” Proceedings of the Indiana Academy of Science, vol. 45, 1935, pp. 206–10, journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/ias/article/view/4753.

Waldo, C. A. “What Might Have Been.” Proceedings of the Indiana Academy of Science, vol. 26, 1916, pp. 445–46, journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/ias/article/view/14627.

Gardner, Elizabeth K. “Purdue Professor Protected Pi from Half-Baked Bill in 1800s.” Purdue University News, 14 Mar. 2014, www.purdue.edu/newsroom/archive/releases/2014/Q1/purdue-professor-protected-pi-from-half-baked-bill-in-1897.html.

Teacher notes
  • Standards: G.GMD.A.1 (informal arguments for circumference and area), G.SRT.C.8 (Pythagorean theorem), N.Q.A.3 (accuracy).
  • “Squaring the circle” means constructing, with compass and straightedge alone, a square with the same area as a given circle. Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 that π is transcendental, which makes it impossible.
  • Representative Taylor I. Record introduced House Bill 246. The House passed it on February 5, 1897, and Indianapolis papers reported it that day and the next. (Newspapers once published several editions each day.) The bill went directly to the Senate’s Committee on Temperance.
  • The bill implies contradictory values of pi elsewhere in its text (something something cranks); 3.2 is the one most often cited.
  • Waldo claims he was at the statehouse to look after the Indiana Academy of Science’s appropriation; other sources claim he was lobbying for his employer’s budget. The Indianapolis Journal reported a senator’s verdict: the Senate “might as well try to legislate water to run up hill as to establish mathematical truth by law.”
