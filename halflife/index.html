What’s Two Years Over Half a Lifetime?

I’d like to open with a radiocarbon dating joke, but it doesn’t seem age-appropriate. On to business!

Radiocarbon dating is a method for determining (estimating!) the age of an object that contains organic material. We assess it by measuring the amount of radiocarbon, or C-14, in that object.

C-14 is created in Earth’s atmosphere when cosmic rays interact with nitrogen. This atmospheric C-14 combines with atmospheric oxygen to form radioactive carbon dioxide. That carbon dioxide finds its way into plants via photosynthesis and, when animals eat those plants, they take on some of that carbon themselves.

When an animal or plant dies, it can no longer exchange carbon with its environment, so the fixed amount of radiocarbon in its remains decreases as the C-14 decays. By measuring the amount of radiocarbon in a bone, hunk of wood, or other organic material, scientists can gauge its approximate age. More C-14, more recently living; less C-14, less recently living. Got it? Good.

  1. A bone has 25 percent of its original carbon-14. How many half-lives have passed? How old is the bone if the half-life is 5,568 years? If it is 5,730?
  2. Write a function A(t) for the fraction of carbon-14 left after t years, using a half-life of h years.
  3. A piece of charcoal has 60 percent of its carbon-14 left. Use logarithms to find its age with each half-life. Round to the nearest year.
  4. Divide your two ages in question 3. Do the same for question 1. What do you notice? Why does it happen?

Answers

  1. Two half-lives: 11,136 years with 5,568; 11,460 years with 5,730. That’s a 324 year difference!
  2. A(t) = (1/2)t/h
  3. t = h · log 0.6 ÷ log 0.5. About 4,103 years with 5,568; about 4,223 years with 5,730.
  4. Both ratios equal 5,730 ÷ 5,568, about 1.03. Age is proportional to the half-life; changing the half-life rescales every age by the same factor.

Willard Libby won the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for radiocarbon dating. Willard Libby and his team at U Chicago initially proposed a C-14 half-life value of 5720 ± 47 years (meaning it takes that long for half of the initial quantity of C-14 to decay). Soon after, the number was revised to 5568 ± 30 years and stood as the best estimate for about a decade.

In the early 1960s, researchers agreed that 5,730 ± 40 years was a more accurate value. For simplicity’s sake, and to avoid dating conflicts with thousands of earlier papers, the 1962 Radiocarbon Conference in Cambridge opted to continue using the Libby half-life of 5568 years despite the error in half-life value. Readers who want the “newer,” more accurate value can simply multiply by 1.03.

Labs continue to rely on the “Libby Value” to this day, but those ages get calibrated against other records, like those from tree rings, which correct for the half-life and for ups and downs in atmospheric C-14.

Teacher notes
  • Standards: F.LE.A.2 (construct exponential functions from a description), F.LE.A.4 (express the solution of an exponential equation as a logarithm), F.IF.C.8b (interpret exponential decay).
  • Question 4 is our pivot point. Since t = h · log(fraction left) ÷ log(1/2), every age is proportional to h. The 1962 and 1965 radiocarbon conferences told readers who wished to convert to multiply by 1.03.
  • In radiocarbon dating, “before present” means before 1950.
  • Even 5,730 has since been refined; recent evaluations put the half-life near 5,700 years. The point stands: labs retain 5,568 per convention.
  • The current calibration curve is IntCal20 (2020). A new one is in the works.
