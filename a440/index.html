Bellringer | Precalculus | Five minutes

A Plus Work?

For much of musical history, the note called A had no standard pitch. (In fact, no note had a standard pitch.) Musical notation was a, like, relative thing, man; it was more about relationships between notes (intervals) than the notes themselves. Singers and musicians would set pitch based on what was familiar or comfortable, so variations from artist to artist, town to town, city to city were commonplace. Far out, man. Far out.

Today, orchestras around the world tune A to roughly 440 hertz: 440 vibrations per second. Whence 440?

  1. Model the tuning A with y = sin(2π · 440t), where t is in seconds. What is the period?
  2. Each octave doubles the frequency. What is the frequency of the A one octave higher? Two octaves lower?
  3. A piano splits each octave into 12 equal half steps, so each half step multiplies the frequency by 21/12. Many Baroque ensembles tune one half step below A440 (don’t get me started on guitarists). What frequency is that?
  4. Some sources call 432 hertz the “natural” A. How many half steps below 440 is 432? What evidence would you want before believing the claim?

Answers

  1. 1/440 of a second; about 0.0023 seconds.
  2. 880 hertz; 110 hertz.
  3. 440 ÷ 21/12, about 415.3 hertz. Baroque groups call it 415.
  4. 12 · log₂(440/432), about 0.32 of a half step. Good evidence would come from historical records and controlled studies.

Today, standard concert pitch sets the A above middle C, or A4, to 440 hertz. Every other note on the chromatic scale is derived from this value.

During the Baroque Era, A4 could fall anywhere between roughly 390 and 480 hertz depending on the tuning fork or organ pipe used. Northern German cathedral organs created brighter tones (with A closer to 480 hertz) ideal for stone structures; French court musicians opted for a warmer A (closer to 390 hertz) that was both easier on their voices and suited for intimate spaces.

The Society of German Natural Scientists and Physicians recommended A440 as the standard in 1834. The French government set A at 435 hertz in 1859. In the 1870s, Italian composer Giuseppe Verdi advocated for 435 hertz; by the 1880s he leaned toward 432 hertz (for practical reasons, though later advocates would blend a considerable quantity of esoteric woo into the mix). A4 continued to vary within and between European countries for the entirety of the 19th century and much of the 20th. Meanwhile in the Anglosphere, the American music industry settled on A4 at 440 hertz sometime in the mid-1920s, and the American Standards Association formally recommended the value in 1936. In the UK, Sir James Swinburne, an electrical engineer and amateur musician, pushed for A440 on a practical basis: Britain’s 439 was a prime number, while 440 was easy to divide and to generate electronically.

The pivotal moment came just prior to the Second World War. In May 1939, the International Federation of the National Standardizing Associations (forerunner of today’s International Organization for Standardization, or ISO) held a conference in London, where each nation’s delegates weighed in. (The Americans and the Swiss mailed in their suggestions.) Pragmatism prevailed, and 440 hertz was unanimously adopted as the international recommendation. The ISO affirmed this suggestion in 1955 and again in 1975.

Today, A440 is common but not universal. Ensembles performing music from earlier eras tend to prefer a lower pitch; orchestras like the New York Philharmonic go a bit higher; New Age believers opt for 432 hertz when they align their chakras and heal their DNA and resonate with the universe (again, there is nothing magical about the 432 hertz pitch).

A larger inquiry beckons: how does a number become a standard? Who gets to decide?

Teacher notes
  • Standards: F.IF.B.4 (key features, including periodicity), F.LE.A.4 (logarithms), F.TF.B.5 (+) (model periodic phenomena), F.BF.B.5 (+) (exponents and logarithms as inverses).
  • Half steps between two pitches: n = 12 · log₂(f/440). One half step below 440 is 415.3 hertz; Juilliard’s Baroque ensemble is named Juilliard415. Cute, huh?
  • The 1939 conference met May 11 and 12 at BBC Broadcasting House under the International Standards Association. ISO’s 1955 document was a recommendation (R 16); ISO 16 replaced it in 1975 and was last confirmed in 2022.
  • Verdi backed 435 for his Requiem and later said a slightly lower pitch, 432, was easier on singers. The label “Verdi tuning” for 432 is a 1980s invention of the Schiller Institute.
  • The National Institute of Standards and Technology has broadcast A440 on radio station WWV since 1936, during minute 2 of every hour except the first hour of each UTC day. Fun to check out if time permits.
