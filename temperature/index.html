Bellringer | Statistics | Five minutes

What’s Hot? What’s Not? Find Out on 98.6 TEMP FM!

German physician Carl Wunderlich established the average healthy human body temperature in 1868, reportedly by compiling over a million measurements from 25,000 people. He set the average at 37 degrees Celsius, which is 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit. Right?

  1. Convert 37°C to Fahrenheit with F = 1.8C + 32. Now suppose Wunderlich’s 37 was rounded to the nearest degree, so the true value could be anywhere from 36.5 to 37.5. Convert those. Does 98.6 deserve its decimal place?
  2. A study published in 1992 reported 700 oral temperatures from 148 healthy adults. Only 56 readings were exactly 98.6°F. What percent is that?
  3. A 2020 Stanford study found that average body temperature in the United States has dropped about 0.03°C per birth decade since the 1800s. How much is that over 15 decades, in Celsius and in Fahrenheit?
  4. Wunderlich’s foot-long thermometers stayed in the armpit for 15 to 20 minutes. The 1992 team used digital thermometers in the mouth. What else would you want to know before comparing their averages?

Answers

  1. 98.6°F. The range runs from 97.7°F to 99.5°F, so the decimal claims a precision the original never had.
  2. 8 percent.
  3. 0.45°C, which is 0.81°F. A change in temperature converts by multiplying by 1.8 only; don’t add 32.
  4. Answers vary: who was measured (ages, sex, health), when (time of day), how (site, instrument, calibration), and how many.

Wunderlich reportedly based his figure on more than a million armpit readings from some 25,000 patients. A 1992 study led by Philip Mackowiak found a mean of 98.2°F and urged that 98.6 be abandoned as a clinical standard. This team even tested one of Wunderlich’s surviving thermometers: it read about 1.5 to 2 degrees Celsius higher than their modern measurements. In 2023, a Stanford study of more than 120,000 patients put the average closer to 97.9°F.

With a real set of temperatures in hand, how strong is the evidence that 98.6 isn’t the average?

Sources

Mackowiak, Philip A., et al. “A Critical Appraisal of 98.6°F, the Upper Limit of the Normal Body Temperature, and Other Legacies of Carl Reinhold August Wunderlich.” JAMA, vol. 268, no. 12, 1992, pp. 1578–80, jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/400116.

Protsiv, Myroslava, et al. “Decreasing Human Body Temperature in the United States since the Industrial Revolution.” eLife, vol. 9, 2020, e49555, https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.49555.

Bai, Nina. “Normal Body Temperature Is Personal, Stanford Medicine Researchers Find.” Stanford Medicine News Center, 5 Sept. 2023, med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2023/09/body-temperature.html.

Kuta, Sarah. “What’s Really the Average Human Body Temperature?” Smithsonian Magazine, 16 Oct. 2023, www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/why-986-degrees-is-not-the-average-human-body-temperature-180983071.

Teacher notes
  • Standards: N.Q.A.1 (units), N.Q.A.3 (accuracy), S.ID.A.2 (center and spread), S.ID.C.7 (rate of change in context), S.IC.B.3 (+) and S.IC.B.6 (+) (study design; evaluating reports). AP Statistics (Fall 2026 CED): Topics 1.10 to 1.13 on collecting data.
  • Question 1 is a hypothetical: no one knows exactly how Wunderlich rounded. The point is significant figures; 37 has two and 98.6 has three. JAMA 1992 gives Wunderlich’s reported range as 36.2°C to 37.5°C.
  • The 1992 sample was 148 adults aged 18 to 40: 122 men and 26 women. Readings ranged from 96.0°F to 100.8°F; the authors proposed 99.9°F as the upper limit of normal.
  • The 2020 study (Protsiv and colleagues, eLife) combined Union Army veterans’ records (1862 to 1930), a national survey from the 1970s, and Stanford patients from 2007 to 2017.
  • Allen Shoemaker’s 1996 Journal of Statistics Education dataset gives 130 temperatures reconstructed from the 1992 study’s histogram (mean 98.25°F). Raw and reconstructed data are not alike in dignity.
