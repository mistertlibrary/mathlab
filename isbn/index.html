X Marks the Spot

Find a book in our library collection published before 2007 (it won’t be very hard) and locate its 10-digit ISBN (check the copyright page). Some International Standard Book Numbers end with an X. Why would a number end in a letter?

  1. To check a 10-digit ISBN, multiply its digits by 10, 9, 8, and so on down to 1, then add. A valid ISBN gives a sum divisible by 11. Check the paperback of T. W. Körner’s The Pleasures of Counting (a certified hood classic): 0-521-56823-4.
  2. The hardcover edition starts 0-521-56087. What last digit would make the sum divisible by 11?
  3. Swap two neighboring digits that differ in the paperback’s ISBN, then check again. Will a swap like that ever pass? Why?
  4. Since 2007, ISBNs have 13 digits and a different check: weights of 1 and 3, and a sum divisible by 10. Why will a 13-digit ISBN never need an X?

Answers

  1. The sum is 176 = 16 × 11. Valid, very valid, just like you!
  2. The first nine digits give 166, one more than a multiple of 11. Only a last digit of 10 reaches the next multiple, 176, and a digit can’t be 10. The ISBN is 0-521-56087-X.
  3. Never. Swapping neighbors a and b changes the sum by the difference of the two digits, which can’t be a multiple of 11 when they’re different.
  4. Dividing by 10 leaves a remainder from 0 to 9, so the check digit is always a single digit.

In the 1960s, after the bookseller W. H. Smith planned a computerized warehouse, statistician Gordon Foster designed a nine-digit Standard Book Number for the British and Irish book trade. The trade adopted it in 1967, and the International Organization for Standardization approved a 10-digit version, the ISBN, in 1970.

Dividing by 11 catches every single wrong digit and every swap of two digits, but sometimes it calls for a check digit of 10, so the standard uses X, the Roman numeral for ten. On January 1, 2007, ISBNs switched to 13 digits, and the X disappeared.

We did lose something when we dropped X from the book trade. Which mistakes can a 13-digit ISBN miss?

Teacher notes
  • Standards: Mathematical Practices 7 (structure) and 8 (repeated reasoning); Computer Science and Design Thinking 8.1.12.DA.2.
  • Question 3 generalizes: dividing by 11 catches a swap of any two different digits, adjacent or not.
  • The teaser’s answer: with weights 1 and 3, swapping neighbors that differ by 5 (0 and 5, 1 and 6, and so on) goes undetected. Making Numbers Count by Chip Heath and Karla Starr (ISBN 9781982165444) still passes if you swap its 1 and 6.
  • Library extension: we can let students hunt in the stacks or the digital catalog for an X. Each format of a book gets its own ISBN, which is why Körner’s paperback and hardcover differ.
  • Converting to 13 digits: add 978 and recompute the check digit. 0-521-56087-X becomes 978-0-521-56087-0.
