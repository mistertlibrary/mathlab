Bellringer | Algebra 1 | Five minutes

Who Counted to 10,000?

Many phones and fitness trackers set a 10,000-step daily goal. Why?

  1. Let’s estimate that roughly 2,000 steps constitute a mile. Write an equation for miles m in terms of steps s. How many miles is 10,000 steps?
  2. A 2019 study of 16,741 women, ages 62 to 101, found that the health benefit of walking more (measured as lower death rates, unequivocally the best health benefit) leveled off at about 7,500 steps a day. What’s the difference between this quantity and the default 10,000? Represent the answer as a percentage of 10,000.
  3. Sketch the shape the study describes, with steps on the x-axis and benefits on the y-axis. Is it a straight line?
  4. The study looked at women ages 62 to 101. Does its finding apply to you? How might you find out?

Answers

  1. m = s ÷ 2,000. 10,000 steps is 5 miles.
  2. 2,500 fewer steps; 25 percent fewer.
  3. It rises, then levels off. Not a straight line.
  4. Not necessarily. A study tells us about the people it studied.

A sketch of the shape the study describes, not its data.

So, where does the 10,000-step goal come from? The study’s authors write that it likely comes from the name of a pedometer sold in Japan in 1965: the manpo-kei, or “10,000 steps meter.” Why 10,000? That’s a subject of debate we don’t have time for right now.

Instead: who decided you need eight glasses of water a day?

Sources

Calechman, Steve. “10,000 Steps a Day, or Fewer?” Harvard Health Publishing, 11 July 2019, www.health.harvard.edu/blog/10000-steps-a-day-or-fewer-2019071117305.

Lee, I-Min, et al. “Association of Step Volume and Intensity with All-Cause Mortality in Older Women.” JAMA Internal Medicine, vol. 179, no. 8, 2019, pp. 1105–12, https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.0899.

Maldarelli, Claire. “Why 10,000 Steps a Day Isn’t the Secret to Better Health.” Popular Science, 26 Apr. 2021, www.popsci.com/story/health/10000-steps-evidence-study.

Teacher notes
  • Standards: F.LE.A.1 (recognize a quantity changing at a constant rate), F.IF.B.4 (sketch key features from a verbal description), percent change. Question 4 is a research habit: who was studied?
  • The study measured deaths among 16,741 women, average age 72, in the Women's Health Study. Benefits appeared by about 4,400 steps a day and leveled off near 7,500.
  • Why 10,000? Accounts differ: the Japanese character for 10,000 (万) is said to resemble a person walking; other retellings credit a Tokyo physician's recommendation or the fitness boom after the 1964 Olympics. The study's authors say only that the number likely comes from the pedometer's name.
  • 2,000 steps per mile is stated as an assumption in question 1, because stride length varies.
