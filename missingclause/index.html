Case file | Algebra 1

The Case of the Missing Clause

Or,
Turns Out This Algebra Stuff Has Real-World Consequences and We’re Learning It for a Reason

Briefing

We’ve all heard the gentle remonstrances of the hydro homies among us: “You should drink eight glasses of water a day!” But where did this advice come from? Who, exactly, is encouraging us to glut our squishy bodies with dihydrogen monoxide?

The year was 2002; the skeptic, Heinz Valtin, a physiologist at Dartmouth Medical School. He went prying into the past to trace the eight-a-day admonition to its source.

Since we’re in school, your job is, of course, to follow in his footsteps, doing exactly what he has already done in the hope of better understanding both basic algebra and the way certain factoids travel through our information ecosystem.

Each exhibit below offers one piece of evidence and poses a few problems. If you successfully solve the first, the second will open, and so on and so forth. As you proceed, log each source you complete.

What prize dangles from the end of this rope? Time will tell!

The Rule

Have you seen this rule before? Of course you have. But what’s missing? What does a diligent researcher want to find alongside every claim?

“Drink at least eight 8-ounce glasses of water a day.”

Source: none given.

Trail log | Exhibit A
  1. How many fluid ounces are in eight 8-ounce glasses?
  2. CODE How many milliliters is that?
  3. The rule says “at least.” Write an inequality for w, the milliliters of water the 8-glass rule mandates each day.
Hint

Multiply the number of glasses by the milliliters in a single glass. Remember that “at least” means the value can be equal to or greater than the value you calculate.

Exhibit B2004

The Experts

The year was 2004; the place, the Institute of Medicine. A hot new hydration report dropped, reporting how much water categorically “healthy” people actually consumed each day. Well-hydrated women took in about 2.7 liters total; well-hydrated men about 3.7 liters.

Here’s the rub: “total” counts every possible source of precious moisture. It includes plain old regular fish-live-in-it water, other beverages (like tea and coffee), and food.

Have you ever heard about the 80/20 rule, formally titled the Pareto principle? It states that roughly 80% of consequences stem from 20% of causes. 80% of sales, for example, come from 20% of clients; 80% of wealth is held by 20% of the population; 80% of worker output (say, in a group project) comes from 20% of the laborers. While this sort of distribution is a generalization, it’s often a good rule of thumb. But does it apply to hydration?

Turns out about 80% of water comes from drinks while about 20% comes from food. Same numbers, different idea: this is a split between two sources, not a few causes producing most of the effects. The IOM panel’s news release ran under the cheeky headline “Let Thirst Be Your Guide.”

Sources

Institute of Medicine. Dietary Reference Intakes for Water, Potassium, Sodium, Chloride, and Sulfate. National Academies Press, 2005, www.nationalacademies.org/read/10925/chapter/6.

“Let Thirst Be Your Guide: Report Sets Dietary Intake Levels for Water, Salt, and Potassium to Maintain Health and Reduce Chronic Disease Risk.” ScienceDaily, 12 Feb. 2004, www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/02/040212092439.htm.

Trail log | Exhibit B
  1. Convert 2.7 liters to milliliters.
  2. CODE Food supplies about 20 percent of total water. Let d represent total milliliters of water coming from drinks. Write and solve an equation: food water + drink water = total water.
  3. How many 8-ounce glasses of drinks is that? (Round to the nearest tenth.) The IOM panel counts all manner of beverages. Does Exhibit A specify which drinks count?
Hint

20 percent of 2,700 is 0.20 × 2,700.

Exhibit C1974

Suspect One | The Professor

Obituaries: biographies that go uncontested by their subjects. Shame we don’t have more control over how the world remembers us!

When Harvard nutritionist Fredrick J. Stare passed in 2002, one obituary called him “an early champion of drinking at least six glasses of water a day.” (Bonus points: can you write a quippier version of the same line?)

Later, a colleague of the deceased found this passage in Nutrition for Good Health, a 1974 book Stare co-wrote with Margaret McWilliams:

“How much water each day? This is usually well regulated by various physiological mechanisms, but for the average adult, somewhere around 6 to 8 glasses per 24 hours and this can be in the form of coffee, tea, milk, soft drinks, beer, etc. Fruits and vegetables are also good sources of water.”

Sources

Stare, Fredrick J., and Margaret McWilliams. Nutrition for Good Health. Plycon, 1974, p. 175. Quoted in Valtin.

Sherrill, Philip. “Nutrition Department Crusader Dead at 91.” The Harvard Crimson, 9 Apr. 2002, www.thecrimson.com/article/2002/4/9/nutrition-department-crusader-dead-at-91.

Valtin, Heinz. “‘Drink at Least Eight Glasses of Water a Day.’ Really? Is There Scientific Evidence for ‘8 × 8’?” American Journal of Physiology: Regulatory, Integrative and Comparative Physiology, vol. 283, no. 5, 2002, pp. R993–R1004, https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002.

Trail log | Exhibit C
  1. Rewrite the book’s advice as a compound inequality for g, the number of glasses. Write Exhibit A’s rule as an inequality, too.
  2. Graph both on the same number line. Which whole numbers of glasses satisfy both suggestions?
  3. CODE Convert the book’s range to fluid ounces. The code is the low end followed by the high end.
  4. Can you specify two ways the book’s advice differs from Exhibit A?
Hint

“Somewhere around 6 to 8” sits between two numbers: 6 ≤ g ≤ 8. How would you depict “at least eight”?

Exhibit D1945

Suspect Two | The Board

The year was 1945; the body, the National Research Council’s Food and Nutrition Board. It was time to revise dietary recommendations for Americans.

Valtin (remember Valtin?) found that another researcher, P. Thomas, suspected the eight-glass rule began with this passage:

“A suitable allowance of water for adults is 2.5 liters daily in most instances. An ordinary standard for diverse persons is 1 milliliter for each calorie of food.”

The passage continues! You will need this exhibit’s code to read the rest.

Sources

Food and Nutrition Board, National Academy of Sciences. Recommended Dietary Allowances, Revised 1945. National Research Council, Aug. 1945. Reprint and Circular Series 122. Quoted in Valtin.

Valtin, Heinz. “‘Drink at Least Eight Glasses of Water a Day.’ Really? Is There Scientific Evidence for ‘8 × 8’?” American Journal of Physiology: Regulatory, Integrative and Comparative Physiology, vol. 283, no. 5, 2002, pp. R993–R1004, https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002.

Trail log | Exhibit D
  1. The Board’s standard is 1 milliliter for each calorie of food. Write a function W(c) for the milliliters of water for c calories. Complete a table for 1,500; 2,000; 2,500; and 3,000 calories. What is the slope? What does it suggest?
  2. Solve W(c) = 1,896, the milliliters specified by Exhibit A’s rule. What does your answer mean? What calorie level matches the Board’s 2.5 liters?
  3. CODE Convert the Board’s 2.5 liters to 8-ounce glasses (round to the nearest tenth). Enter the digits without the decimal point (for 12.3, enter 123).
Hint

Each calorie adds 1 mL, so W(c) = c. Do you remember what divisor we use to turn milliliters into glasses?

Exhibit E1945

The Missing Clause

Here is the Board’s full passage quoted in Valtin:

“A suitable allowance of water for adults is 2.5 liters daily in most instances. An ordinary standard for diverse persons is 1 milliliter for each calorie of food. Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods.”

Emphasis added.

Valtin credits Thomas with this explanation of what happened next:

“Thomas suggests that the last sentence was not heeded, and the recommendation was therefore erroneously interpreted as eight glasses of water to be drunk each day.”

Sources

Food and Nutrition Board, National Academy of Sciences. Recommended Dietary Allowances, Revised 1945. National Research Council, Aug. 1945. Reprint and Circular Series 122. Quoted in Valtin.

Valtin, Heinz. “‘Drink at Least Eight Glasses of Water a Day.’ Really? Is There Scientific Evidence for ‘8 × 8’?” American Journal of Physiology: Regulatory, Integrative and Comparative Physiology, vol. 283, no. 5, 2002, pp. R993–R1004, https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002.

Trail log | Exhibit E
  1. “Most” means more than half. Write an inequality for f, the milliliters of the Board’s 2.5 liters that come from food.
  2. Write an inequality for d, the milliliters left to drink.
  3. CODE Convert d to glasses (you guessed it: round to the nearest tenth). Enter the digits without the decimal point.
  4. Does the 1945 Board suggestion support “eight glasses of water a day”? Answer in one sentence. Make sure that sentence includes a number!
Hint

What’s half of 2,500? If food supplies more than that, what’s left to drink?

Bureau Bonus | The Retellings

Have you ever played Telephone or Whisper Down the Alley? Have you ever heard the same story from two or more sources? Stories are fickle things that change with each retelling, especially when they center on what’s essentially medical advice.

Check three retellings of the eight-glass rule against your evidence.

  1. A 2026 article says: “Two and a half liters works out to roughly eight eight-ounce glasses.” Check the math.
  2. A 2025 article dates the Board’s recommendation to 1941. Valtin cites a revision dated August 1945. Which date has better evidence behind it? Why?
  3. A 2022 news story quotes the Board: “A suitable allowance of water for adults is usually 2.5 litres daily. Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods.” Compare it word by word with Exhibit E. What changed? Does the change matter?
